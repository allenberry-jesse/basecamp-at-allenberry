The Concept
Investor Thesis

The hardest amenity to build is already running.

For half a century, growing old in America has meant choosing between two unhappy ends of a single spectrum. At one end, the institution — the care facility, the wing walled off from ordinary life. At the other, an idealized vision of staying put, the private home kept at all costs, where independence too often curdles into isolation. Most people fear the first and quietly drift toward the second.

Basecamp is built on the premise that there is a third way.

That third way is aging in community. Where aging in place fixes on the dwelling, aging in community shifts the emphasis to relationships — the neighbors, family, and shared daily life that research consistently links to aging well. It is also deliberately multi-generational, because being surrounded only by people your own age is its own quiet form of isolation.

The market gap is real and growing. Existing housing is built largely for nuclear families or for institutional eldercare, with little in between. The opportunity is to build the missing category: a multi-generational community organized around life transitions rather than life stage, anchored by a hospitality property that already produces the most valuable amenity any residential developer can offer — programmed daily life.

The housing form has a name. Missing middle, coined by architect Daniel Parolek, describes house-scale multi-unit buildings — cottages, courtyard residences, multiplexes, ADU-paired homes — that occupy the long-vanished space between detached single-family homes and large apartment blocks. Zoned out of existence in the postwar era, they are only now being rebuilt as the country reckons with shrinking households, an aging population, and a deepening affordability gap.

Basecamp adopts this typology and designs each home to be lived in across stages — flexible layouts, primary-plus-suite configurations, optional in-home elevators, and discreet smart-home and telehealth infrastructure that let a residence adapt to its occupants. Each home is also designed to be held across generations: a family's investment in one chapter becomes a foothold for the next.

The result is a continuum of support within a single community — from full independence to as much help as life eventually requires, so that needing more never means leaving.

The Principles

Six commitments shape every decision.

i.

Inclusive

All ages, all abilities, elders especially. No wing, no waiting room, no segregation by stage of life.
ii.

Interdependent

Reciprocity over independence. Neighbors rely on neighbors, across generations, as a matter of design rather than charity.
iii.

Engaged

A daily invitation to participate, learn, perform, and contribute.
iv.

Healthy

Wellness of mind, body, and spirit, with an honest readiness for the harder chapters of disease, disability, and the end of life.
v.

Enabling

Universal design throughout, with discreet technology where it helps. Homes, paths, and gathering spaces that work as easily for a wheelchair as for a toddler.
vi.

Human-scaled

Clustered into walkable, village-sized neighborhoods, cars kept to the edges and daily life on foot. Small enough that people actually know one another.

Looking After Our Own

A community, not an institution.

Care begins with neighbors. Someone checks in. A meal arrives when a resident is recovering. A ride gets organized. This is the first and most reliable layer of support — the reciprocal everyday kind that no facility can buy and most communities have forgotten how to provide.

Around it, a professional concierge care layer that scales with need. Tap into our network of preferred providers and visiting professionals (nurses, therapists, geriatric specialists) who circulate on a regular schedule. Professionals are contracted directly by residents with support through the resort's wellness operations and connected-health infrastructure built into every home.

Because support scales in place, a change in health rarely means a change in address. The hard infrastructure of care — the people who provide it, the schedule it runs on — is woven into daily life rather than walled off into a facility wing.

The Asset
Structure of Ownership

An appreciating asset, in more than one currency.

The conventional model for residential care is a service contract. Continuing-care retirement communities serve real needs and many families well, but the model asks households to convert significant assets into ongoing care services, leaving little to pass on.

Basecamp is structured differently, and it appreciates in two currencies.

Financial Capital

Owned, not rented against care.

The ownership works three ways. Residences appreciate. They earn: when an owner is traveling or not yet in residence, Allenberry places the home in a professionally managed rental pool, turning the resort's year-round demand into income. And they pass on: a residence can be handed down, reoccupied at another life stage, or sold back into the community. A parent's later years and a grandchild's first home can happen under the same roof, a generation apart.

Social Capital

The residents are the bricks.

The relationships between them are the mortar. Together they build the one thing institutional eldercare actively destroys: a reservoir of trust, reciprocity, and belonging that compounds quietly over years. It is the asset that makes the financial one worth having.
